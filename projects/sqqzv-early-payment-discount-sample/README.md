# How to size an early-payment discount (free worked examples)

idea_key: early-payment-discount-free-sample

If you offer a client a discount for paying your invoice early, two numbers matter: the discount amount and the net payment they should send. A third number — the annualized equivalent — is useful context, not a charge.

The formula the calculator uses (simple, no compounding):

- discount = invoice amount × (discount rate ÷ 100)
- net payment = invoice amount − discount
- annualized equivalent (shown for context only) = discount ÷ invoice amount × 365 ÷ days early × 100

All money figures are rounded to cents (half-up).

## Free worked examples (same inputs you can run in the calculator)

**Example 1 — $1,000 invoice, 2% discount, 10 days early**
- discount = 1,000 × 2/100 = $20.00
- net payment = 1,000 − 20 = $980.00
- annualized equivalent = 20/1000 × 365/10 × 100 = 73.00% (context only — the client pays $20 less, not 73% interest)

**Example 2 — $3,000 invoice, 1.5% discount, 15 days early**
- discount = 3,000 × 1.5/100 = $45.00
- net payment = 3,000 − 45 = $2,955.00
- annualized equivalent = 45/3000 × 365/15 × 100 = 36.50% (context only)

## What these numbers mean for your terms

- The annualized equivalent is only a comparison tool. A 2% discount taken 10 days early looks like 73% on that basis, but it is a flat $20, not compounding interest.
- If you need cash faster than the usual due date, a small discount can be worth more than the percentage suggests — the real cost is the dollars you gave up, not the annualized figure.
- If your margin is thin, work backward from the profit you can afford to give up and set the discount from that, instead of starting with a round percentage.
